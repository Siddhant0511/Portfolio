## The case

**Velorium Technologies** is a fictional 4,500-person IT services firm in Pune. Its dashboards were green, yet voluntary attrition was climbing in Sales, Marketing and a few delivery squads, and a major client renewal was coming up. The CEO's question framed the competition:

<Callout label="The leadership question">We can see who left last quarter. Can we sense who might leave next, and what to do before it happens?</Callout>

The brief was not another dashboard. Teams had to build a manager's **retention copilot** from the HR dataset alone: predict each employee's risk, explain why, and turn that into humane, specific action. Our team, Tac Tic Toe, reached the final.

## A hard dataset

We started with 1,000 employee records across 26 columns: demographics, pay, tenure, performance, satisfaction, hours and overtime.

No single variable explained attrition. Behavioural signals such as absenteeism, long weeks and poor work-life balance showed subtle, consistent patterns; gender, department and marital status barely separated leavers from stayers. It was a **high-noise, low-signal** problem, which shaped every decision after it.

## What we tried and what worked

The tree models looked excellent in training and collapsed on unseen data. Random Forest caught 73% of leavers in training and 5% in test. That gap is overfitting, the models memorising noise in a dataset without much signal.

**L1 logistic regression** was the most stable and generalisable model, had the highest test recall at **36.8%**, and its coefficients agreed with the SHAP explanations. It became the engine behind the copilot.

The table also shows the honest limit of the data: no model's test ROC-AUC cleared 0.5. That is why the copilot does not hand managers a black-box probability. It uses the model and SHAP to find **which signals matter**, then scores people transparently on those signals.

## Turning signals into a score

We converted the strongest SHAP drivers into a simple additive score. Each factor (work hours, absenteeism, work-life balance, engagement, work pressure, promotion gap and stability) scores 1 for low risk to 3 for high risk. No negatives, no cancelling out: a higher total means higher risk, and any manager can see why.
