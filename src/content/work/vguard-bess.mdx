## The brief

The V-Guard Big Idea competition asked teams to propose a new business for **V-Guard Industries**, a company built on voltage stabilisers, inverters and power electronics. Our team, Tac Tic Toe, proposed **battery energy storage systems (BESS)**.

A business plan like this is easy to write with market-size slides. We wanted the case to rest on the grid itself, so the plan started with data: **15-minute generation records from India's National Load Despatch Centre (NLDC)** for four representative days across a full year.

## Reading the grid

The four days show a grid that never sits still. Demand, solar and thermal all move differently by season, which means no storage plan sized for one quarter works for the other three.

## Sizing storage from the curve

The grid's highest-leverage move is to flatten thermal generation into a constant line. That means batteries **absorb the midday solar surplus** and **discharge it into the morning and evening peaks**. We measured the energy that has to move for each quarter.

The result was surprisingly stable: **152 to 156 GWh a day**, whatever the season. The complication is efficiency. At a realistic **85% round-trip efficiency**, every day loses 26.9 to 27.6 GWh in conversion alone. That energy has to be generated, not just stored.

## Why V-Guard has a right to play

A battery system's value is not in the cell. The **Battery Management System** and **Power Conversion System** are close descendants of the inverter electronics V-Guard has made for two decades: DC/AC conversion, thermal management and control firmware.

<Figure title="Where the hardware value sits" note="Share of hardware revenue in the SBU model.">
  <Bars

V-Guard builds the 38% it already knows how to make, and buys the cell, the part most exposed to global commodity swings, through a strategic import partnership protected by customs duty exemptions. It is not becoming a battery company; it is extending a power-electronics franchise around a commoditised cell.

## The plan: start small, earn the right to bid big

We proposed a phased **business-to-business-to-government** sequence. Commercial and industrial (C&I) customers come first because each deployment pays for itself and builds the commissioned track record that utility tenders require before V-Guard can bid.

## Beating diesel on physics and cost

The first customers already run diesel generators for backup. The pitch had to beat them on the two things those customers care about.

<Compare
  beforeLabel="500 kW diesel generator"
  afterLabel="500 kW BESS"
  rows={[
    { k: 'Upfront price', before: '₹35-40 lakh', after: '₹65-75 lakh, removed by Energy-as-a-Service' },
    { k: 'Cost per kWh delivered', before: '~₹44.65 fully burdened, ₹25-28 in fuel alone', after: '₹13.67-23.41' },
    { k: '10-year ownership cost', before: '₹95 lakh to ₹1.1 Cr+', after: '₹45-60 lakh' },
    { k: 'Switchover time', before: '10-30 seconds, mechanical start-up', after: 'Under 20 milliseconds, solid-state' },
    { k: 'Emissions', before: 'Restricted in NCR under GRAP', after: 'Zero local emissions' },
  ]}
/>

For a data centre, switchover speed is the whole argument. A diesel generator's 10 to 30 second gap is long enough to trip equipment; a sag of 50 milliseconds can reset controllers. The plan put numbers on that: unplanned downtime averages **$9,000 per minute**, and a single micro-outage on a 64,000-GPU training cluster can waste close to **$0.95 million** of compute in checkpoint reloads.

The upfront premium, the biggest objection, is handled with an **Energy-as-a-Service** model: V-Guard or a financing partner owns the asset and the customer pays per kWh, below their blended grid-and-diesel cost. The battery then earns money every day through demand-charge management, time-of-day arbitrage and rooftop solar self-consumption, not only during outages.

## The numbers

We rebuilt the SBU model as a standalone, zero-dividend, 15-year greenfield business (FY27 to FY41), funded 75:25 debt to equity at 8.5% and 12%.
