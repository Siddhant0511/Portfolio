## The brief

**Aquila Projects**, the company in the course case, needed a category strategy for **butterfly valves**, part of its pipes, valves and fittings spend, covering FY2027 to FY2029 and sponsored by its Chief Procurement Officer.

The category was chosen as a pilot not for its size, but because it exposes every structural weakness in how the company buys. The deliverable was not a sourcing event. It was a strategy: what to lock in, where to standardise, and which trade-offs to accept consciously for three years.

## One category, two personalities

The first finding reframed everything else. What was managed as one category behaved like two, bundled together by habit.

Different design codes, legacy vendor preferences and inconsistent ERP naming across business units meant the "one category" had no shared logic at all. The decision: split it formally into two sub-segments with separate playbooks, and unify naming and design codes.

## Where the money leaks

- **SKU proliferation on a small core.** A standardisation opportunity hiding in plain sight.
- **An informal duopoly with no structure.** Spend was already concentrated, but without long-term agreements or consistent pricing, even from the same supplier.
- **Behavioural pricing.** Prices tracked urgency, lot size and negotiation timing more than steel prices; the 150 mm diameter showed price growth that no specification change explained.
- **Predictable "emergencies".** Emergency orders spiked every Q3 and Q4, driven by late engineering freezes and budget deadlines. Internal audits confirmed it was a pattern, not an exception.
- **Over-specification.** Marginal "project-specific" differences such as SS304 against SS316 or coating thickness inflated cost, and engineering often named suppliers before procurement was involved.

<Callout>Supplier power here was largely self-inflicted: internal fragmentation, not the market, was handing suppliers the leverage.</Callout>

The supply market has three tiers: global OEMs with deep capability and premium prices, large Indian manufacturers that can serve both segments, and regional fabricators that compete hard on standard valves but lack critical-duty certification. Capacity runs at 75-85%, moderately tight rather than short, and several suppliers have announced Indian expansions, which opens a window to lock in agreements before the balance shifts.

The structure-conduct-performance view pointed the same way: the market structure favours Aquila more than its results suggest, so the fix is governance and price transparency, not waiting for the market.

The trade-offs were stated openly: less project-level customisation, slower and more deliberate supplier switching, and fewer but larger commitments to strategic suppliers, in exchange for removing avoidable price variance and urgency premiums. The CEO's closing test for the strategy was what Aquila would consciously **stop** doing:

- Letting engineering freeze vendor names before procurement is involved
- Treating the Q3-Q4 surge as an exception instead of a plannable pattern
- Letting marginal spec differences drive separate sourcing for each project
- Negotiating each project in isolation instead of as one demand stream
